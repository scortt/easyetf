# Why Is the Nasdaq-100 So Damn Good?

![Why Is the Nasdaq-100 So Damn Good?](../assets/nasdaq-100-vs-berkshire.png)

Over the last ten years, Berkshire Hathaway has compounded at roughly **13% a year**.

That's excellent.

There is just one problem.

**The Nasdaq-100 did better.**

And not by hiring a better Warren Buffett.

It has no fund manager.

No investment committee.

No analysts building valuation models.

It is just an index.

So perhaps the interesting question isn't:

> **Is Warren Buffett outdated?**

The more interesting question is:

> **Why is the Nasdaq-100 so damn good?**

---

## Maybe ten years is just luck

Fine.

Ten years isn't that long.

Maybe we just happened to measure during the greatest technology boom in history.

So let's move the starting line.

QQQ launched on **March 10, 1999**.

This is almost comically bad timing.

The dot-com bubble was approaching its peak.

Soon afterward, the Nasdaq would collapse.

Surely Buffett wins now.

Not quite.

From QQQ's launch through 2026, QQQ still produced a higher annualized return than Berkshire.

That is strange.

We deliberately chose one of the worst possible starting points for technology stocks.

The index then lived through the dot-com crash, the global financial crisis, COVID, inflation, aggressive rate hikes, and several bear markets — and still came out ahead.

So maybe this isn't just a ten-year story.

---

## Fine. Let's go all the way back.

QQQ is relatively young.

But the Nasdaq-100 isn't.

The index was created in **1985**.

Now Buffett gets his best years back.

The 1980s.

The 1990s.

Years when Berkshire sometimes returned 50%, 60%, even more than 80%.

Surely *this* changes the picture.

It does.

Over the full period, Berkshire's extraordinary early compounding matters enormously.

But something else becomes visible.

**The Nasdaq-100 is still remarkably close.**

And that's when the comparison stops being about Buffett.

It becomes a question about the index itself.

> **What exactly did we accidentally build in 1985?**

---

## A very strange index

The Nasdaq-100 isn't the American economy.

That's closer to the S&P 500's job.

It isn't really a technology index either.

There is no rule saying:

> **Buy technology companies.**

Instead, the rule is roughly:

> **Take the largest non-financial companies listed on Nasdaq.**

That sounds almost arbitrary.

And yet look at what this rule has repeatedly captured:

- Microsoft
- Apple
- Amazon
- Alphabet
- Meta
- NVIDIA

The index didn't predict these companies.

It didn't discover them early.

It did something much easier.

**It waited until they proved themselves.**

---

## The Nasdaq doesn't pick winners

This may be the key.

Traditional investing asks:

> **Which company will win?**

The Nasdaq-100 asks a different question:

> **Which companies are already winning?**

That difference is enormous.

You didn't need to predict Amazon in 1997.

You didn't need to understand the iPhone before Apple built it.

You didn't need to predict cloud computing.

You didn't need to understand CUDA before NVIDIA became important.

The companies did the hard part.

They competed.

They survived.

They grew.

And eventually the index increased its exposure to them.

The Nasdaq-100 is not very good at predicting winners.

**It may be very good at harvesting winners.**

---

## And it quietly gets rid of losers

This is the other half of the mechanism.

A human investor can fall in love with a stock.

An index can't.

A fund manager can spend years defending a bad thesis.

An index doesn't care.

Companies shrink.

Their weights fall.

Eventually some leave the index.

The portfolio continuously replaces yesterday's declining companies with today's growing ones.

This creates a strange property:

**The companies inside an index grow old.**

**The index itself doesn't.**

---

## Three starting lines, three different stories

The result changes depending on where you start measuring.

### 1. The most recent decade

Berkshire has compounded at roughly **13% a year** over the last ten years.

The Nasdaq-100 has been substantially stronger.

That makes Buffett look old-fashioned.

### 2. Since QQQ launched in 1999

QQQ launched close to the peak of the dot-com bubble — almost the worst possible starting point for a technology-heavy index.

And yet from launch through 2026, QQQ still came out ahead of Berkshire on an annualized basis.

That makes the Nasdaq look even more impressive.

### 3. Since the Nasdaq-100 was created in 1985

Move the starting line back far enough to include Buffett's extraordinary 1980s and 1990s, and Berkshire's relative performance improves dramatically.

The gap becomes much tighter.

That is important.

A strategy can look dominant or mediocre simply because the starting line moved.

---

## What about a normal investor who keeps adding money?

There is another problem with comparing lump-sum returns.

Most people do not wake up in 1985 with a giant pile of cash and then never earn another dollar.

They get paid.

They save.

They invest.

Then they do it again.

So consider a simple historical simulation:

- Start in 1986
- Invest **$1,000 every month**
- Never market-time
- Never sell
- Reinvest distributions
- Continue through the end of 2025

Total contributions:

**$480,000.**

Approximate ending values:

| Investment | Ending value | Annualized IRR |
|---|---:|---:|
| **Nasdaq-100 Total Return** | **~$19.5M** | **~14.4%** |
| **Berkshire Hathaway** | **~$17.9M** | **~14.1%** |
| **S&P 500 Total Return** | **~$7.9M** | **~11.4%** |

Forty years.

One of history's greatest investors.

One rules-based index.

And the result is almost a tie.

That is much stranger than QQQ beating Berkshire over the last ten years.

---

## Buffett and Nasdaq solve the same problem in opposite ways

Buffett is extraordinary because he has spent decades doing something almost no human can do consistently:

**Allocate capital toward great businesses.**

The Nasdaq-100 approaches the same problem from the opposite direction.

It doesn't try to be brilliant.

It builds a system where successful companies gradually become more important and unsuccessful ones gradually become less important.

No genius required.

Just rules.

Buffett's answer is:

> **Identify great businesses. Value them. Wait for the right price.**

The Nasdaq's answer is:

> **Let companies prove they are winners, then systematically own more of them.**

One depends on extraordinary human judgment.

The other depends on a mechanism.

Over roughly four decades, both produced extraordinary results.

---

## So why is the Nasdaq-100 so good?

Probably not because it is a magical technology index.

Probably not because it can predict innovation.

And probably not because valuation no longer matters.

A more interesting explanation is that it combines several powerful properties:

1. **It is biased toward companies that have already achieved enormous scale.**
2. **It excludes financial companies, leaving more room for asset-light growth businesses.**
3. **Market-cap weighting naturally gives more weight to companies whose equity value keeps rising.**
4. **Reconstitution and rebalancing gradually reduce exposure to fading companies.**
5. **It lets new winners enter without requiring investors to predict them years in advance.**
6. **Many of its largest constituents benefit from software economics, network effects, intellectual property, and global scalability.**

The result is something unusual:

A portfolio that is concentrated enough to capture extraordinary winners, but rules-based enough to keep replacing them.

That may be the real story.

Not:

> **Is Warren Buffett outdated?**

But:

> **Why is the Nasdaq-100 so damn good?**

---

## Methodology note

This article is educational research, not investment advice.

Several comparisons use different historical data availability:

- QQQ began trading on March 10, 1999.
- The Nasdaq-100 index was launched in 1985.
- Historical Nasdaq-100 total-return monthly data are not uniformly available for the entire 1985–2025 period.
- The dollar-cost-averaging figures above are therefore **approximate historical simulations**, with part of the early Nasdaq history reconstructed from annual return data.
- Berkshire Hathaway pays essentially no dividend, so its stock-price return is close to its total return.
- Taxes, transaction costs, and inflation are excluded.

The purpose of the exercise is not decimal-point precision.

It is to understand why a surprisingly simple index has remained competitive with one of the most successful capital allocation records in modern financial history.
